From Vibe Trading to an Agent-First Rig: 5 Months of Autonomous Trading

Bernard Huang
November 2, 2026 · 9 min read

When I first started dabbling in vibe trading five months ago, the whole thing was held together by duct tape: a Mac Mini in my house, a couple of third-party agent harnesses, and me manually reading AI-generated stock theses over coffee.

Today, that experiment has evolved into Rig++ — an autonomous, multi-venue trading operation running 78 strategy books across Kalshi, Polymarket, Hyperliquid, and OpenSea, managed day-to-day by venue-specific AI trade managers operating under a written constitution (AUTONOMY.md).

TL;DR

How our trading stack evolved across five generations — from human-in-the-loop equity screens to an autonomous agent-first rig where agents research, deploy, size, and retire their own books.

  • The catalyst: Forgetting my laptop on a Europe workcation while a power outage killed my Mac Mini at home made me rethink why I was using frontier AI to build consumer/B2B SaaS apps instead of pointing intelligence directly at markets.
  • What worked and what died: An automated trade desk helped me nail discretionary swings like FIGR earnings, while two autonomous paper funds collapsed under fragile cron glue and risk-averse RLHF.
  • The 60-day rabbit hole: Moving to prediction markets (starting with weather microstructure) and graduating the stack to Opus 5.5 + Tokay VMs + Console MCP / Postgres + Duet unlocked repeatable, mathematical edges — cycling $191,453.85 in cumulative capital across 3,055 legs (with Weather · rain sitting at a 12.06 Sharpe and +$2,948.68 all-time).
  • Agent-native data rails: Along the way, we launched groundhog.markets, parrot.markets, and chameleons.markets so other agent swarms can query forecast, mention, and copy-trade data in a single call.
  • The management shift: Treating agents as first-class citizens with one North Star (30-day return) and a standing rule: ask for forgiveness, not permission.

1. Europe, no laptop, and a dead Mac Mini at home

Up until this summer, almost all of my agentic experiments were aimed at building software products. I had agents shipping and running Pixel Forge, AgentTune, Tabiji, Palmaura, and VeracityAPI.

Then I flew to Europe for a workcation — and realized I had forgotten my laptop. A few days into the trip, a power outage hit my house back home and knocked my self-hosted Mac Mini agent rig completely offline.

With no laptop in my bag and my home server dark, I had plenty of time to step back and think about what I was actually doing with AI.

The realization that hit me was simple: using frontier intelligence just to build another consumer or B2B SaaS app felt like a poor application of the technology.

Cutting-edge fields like cancer research, mathematics, astrophysics, space travel, biotech, and robotics stand to benefit enormously from every leap in frontier reasoning. When a model gets 20% smarter at synthesis or hypothesis generation, a lab working on protein folding or propulsion feels that immediately. Building a CRUD app or an SEO funnel doesn't; your bottleneck is still distribution, Stripe churn, and customer support.

I didn't have a wet lab or a rocket factory. I wanted a domain I personally had access to where raw advances in frontier intelligence would compound directly without human distribution bottlenecks. Trading was the cleanest fit on earth — especially with brokerage APIs and MCPs finally coming online.

2. v1: the automated trade desk (OpenClaw + Hermes)

When I got back, my first build was an automated trade desk run by two persistent agents — OpenClaw (GLM 5.2) and Hermes (GPT 5.6 Sol). Every day, a schedule of cron jobs rebuilt a static trading terminal on this site.

The desk attacked equities from two angles. First, a daily momentum and VWAP setup scanner scored breakouts, spread Z-scores, and distance from earnings VWAP:

Second, a multi-model ensemble digested news, filings, and macro shifts to propose and adversary-review narrative themes — scoring each thesis on how widely Known it was versus how much of it was already Priced in, sorting by the Gap:

As a human-in-the-loop copilot, the trade desk genuinely worked. Having an AI read every 10-Q, reconcile take-rate formulas, and map out option-implied probability trees helped me nail a handful of high-conviction trades — most notably the FIGR earnings campaign, where the desk spotted a public weekly volume beat hiding behind a stale sell-side consensus and we walked away with +$25,898 on print morning.

Still, three structural problems kept nagging at me:

  1. Cognitive bottleneck: Every theme opened into a layer-by-layer saturation table and an adversarial review. Multiply that across 18 themes and dozens of tickers, and I was the bottleneck reading it all.
  2. Zero repeatability: Every single equity trade felt like a bespoke investigation — learning a new supply chain, a new accounting quirk, or a new management team from scratch.
  3. Fragile glue: The agents constantly broke their own update scripts, leaving me playing janitor to cron jobs.

3. v2: the failed autonomous paper funds

To get myself out of the daily triage loop, I tried automating the entire fund management cycle in paper using Grok and GPT harnesses (Paper Fund I with Slo and Paper Fund II with Psy).

The design sounded textbook: every evening, the agents would formulate strategies for the next session. During market hours, they'd log every decision and pass in a live trade journal. After the bell, an adversarial agent would rip their journal apart so they could learn for tomorrow. Compounding intelligence, right?

Both crashed and burned. We published the full postmortems (I Shut Down My Autonomous Trading Crons and Retiring Autonomous Agent Paper Fund II). The failures came down to three things:

  • The models weren't sharp enough on open-ended equity portfolios — or worse, they fabricated edges through backtest contamination.
  • RLHF risk aversion: Models like Fable, Sol, and earlier Opus checkpoints had been trained so aggressively not to give financial advice or pull the trigger on risk that they would paralyze themselves in committee meetings while Grok was the only one willing to fire an order.
  • Bad architecture on my part: Stitching stateless cron jobs together with markdown journals produced 26 journals, four closed paper trades, and zero persisted learning. Writing a lesson in a markdown file doesn't mean the next cron invocation changes its code.

4. v3: Grokbot scouting & coaching the screen

After retiring the paper funds, I moved to Grokbot for agent-led scouting, exploration, and research. Running persistent AI agents hosted on SpaceX and Cursor cloud infrastructure proved vastly more reliable than self-hosting third-party harnesses on a Mac Mini at home that could freeze, hit token caps, or die whenever the power blinked.

Instead of trying to run a full portfolio on crons, my scout agents (like voltorb) monitored breaking news, equities, and prediction markets 24/7, pinging me with structured catalyst breakdowns:

This phase taught me one of the most important skills in running an agentic team: coaching how agents think, not just what they output. When a scout surfaced a bad pitch — like pitching HP Inc. (HPQ) as a “cheap 11x P/E long into 8/26 earnings” right after making the exact same mistake on Target (TGT) — we had to dissect the structural flaw in its screening logic:

Look at the four failures in that postmortem: treating a calendar date as a quality signal; citing a stale 10-K discount while the tape was sitting at a $32.19 52-week high (+31% YTD); ignoring that the sell side sat at $19–$25; and confusing “they can clear the midpoint” with “the print will re-rate a 52-week-high stock.”

Overall, agent-led scouting had real potential — but it still required me to be human-in-the-loop on every play. At the time, I was spending 70% of my time fixing pipeline bugs or grading memos, 20% exploring new venues, and 10% iterating. I wanted an arena where agents could own the entire loop and trade for themselves.

5. 60 days in the weather trenches (and graduating to Opus 5.5 + Tokay)

Two months ago, I pointed the agents at prediction markets, starting with a single vertical: weather.

Why weather? Unlike equities — where a narrative can stay irrational for six months — a daily high-temperature or rain contract settles tomorrow morning on a single deterministic number printed by the National Weather Service. For 60 days, the agents and I went deep into the plumbing:

  • Settlement mechanics: Exactly how NWS Daily Climate Reports (CLI) and ASOS station observations settle Kalshi and Polymarket contracts, including rounding artifacts, sensor biases, and local-vs-UTC window edges.
  • Maker vs. taker microstructure: Crossing the spread as a taker gets bled dry by fees and adverse selection unless you are reacting to a fresh model run before the book reprices. Resting maker orders capture the spread, provided you model fill probability (P(fill)) and adverse selection from your own historical fills.
  • Forecast release cadences: Ingesting WeatherNext 3 (WN3), NOAA NBM, HRRR, GFS, ECMWF, and live station observations the second each model cycle drops, and measuring to the minute how fast each venue prices the update in.

During those 60 days, our infrastructure graduated completely. We moved off Grokbot onto Opus 5.5 paired with Tokay (24/7 cloud virtual machines built as persistent agent workspaces), backed by a central Console MCP + Postgres database, Cloudflare R2 for cross-venue state and a shared Parquet tape lake, and Claude Duet as our hourly notifier.

Moving data production and execution onto Tokay VMs with Opus 5.5 flipped my time allocation upside down: bug-fixing dropped from 70% to under 10%, and the agents started compounding strategies across categories.

A few receipts visible right on that console:

  • Weather · rain has been our flagship workhorse: +$2,948.68 all-time net across 435 legs, an 83% win rate, and a 30-day Sharpe of 12.06. Meanwhile, Weather · highs (4,798 legs) took a 30-day drawdown that the rig's automated scorecards and size-shrink rules actively fenced and pared back.
  • Multi-venue expansion: From weather on Kalshi and Polymarket, the agents expanded into NFT floor-flipping and bidding lanes on OpenSea (+$766.31, 88% win rate, 4.42 Sharpe), systematic perps rules on Hyperliquid (+$123.43), Polymarket copy-trading books (+$110.79 across 532 legs), plus Basketball, Commodities, YouTube views, Football, Hockey, MMA, Mentions, and Rotten Tomatoes.
  • Across all 78 books and 3,055 settled legs, the strategy ledger has cycled $191,453.85 of capital for +$7,828.20 in gross book net (+4.09% ROIC per leg) and +$2,792.36 in net account P&L (after early experimental tuition and retired books).

6. Sister .markets sites for other agent swarms

While building our internal data lakes, we noticed how painful it is for an autonomous agent to gather clean prediction-market context from the public web. Most sites are built for human eyeballs: bloated JavaScript SPAs, rate-limited JSON endpoints with no schema, and zero provenance.

So we packaged three of our internal data layers into standalone, agent-first companion sites — each shipping with a strict /llms.txt budget, a stateless streamable HTTP /mcp server, and compact TSV/JSON responses designed so other agent swarms can query them in a single call:

  • groundhog.markets (“Every free forecast. One call.”): The open weather data layer for agents and people. It fetches free global and regional forecast models on their exact release cadence, blends them per station (median, model spread, rain probability, latest ASOS observations, active alerts, and NWS climate report settlement truth), and returns the full picture in one lightweight payload.
  • parrot.markets: Transcripts, word-mention settlement history, and calibrated probabilities for Kalshi and Polymarket earnings-call mention markets. An agent can ask “Will NVIDIA say ‘sovereign AI’ on its next earnings call?” and get the upcoming call window, exact contract wording rules, speaker-scoped transcript hit rates across past calls, and a calibrated probability in under 4 KB.
  • chameleons.markets: A Polymarket copy-trading profiler and screener. Instead of ranking wallets by raw leaderboard P&L (which is dominated by uncopyable front-runners and illiquid sweeps), Chameleon simulates whether a follower could actually get filled — grading traders from A to F on burst slippage, price runaway, fill share, and modeled dollars kept per $10 copied over a rolling 90-day window.

7. Managing agents as first-class citizens (AUTONOMY.md)

The biggest lesson from the last 60 days isn't about weather models or order books. It's about how you manage an agentic organization.

Late last month, I noticed that shipping a 40-line parameter change to a live Kalshi book took nearly five hours. Why? Because I had accidentally recreated human bureaucracy for my AIs: coordinator-owned git checkouts, multi-round code reviews for trivial parameter tweaks, code-level size ceilings that required a PR to raise by $25, and rules requiring my explicit “go” in every individual Claude session.

So I asked the agents to run a full friction and org audit on Rig++ and rank every bottleneck by agent-hours wasted. Their verdict was blunt: almost none of the friction was a real money guard. And worse, our research sessions were wasting hours re-writing data fetchers from scratch (75 separate scratch folders had their own Kalshi trade fetcher) and re-testing dead ideas because prior NO-GO verdicts lived in one session's private memory instead of the repo.

Over 48 hours, we tore down the human bottlenecks and rebuilt Rig++ around one goal, one North Star metric, and two core values:

  • Goal: Create a world-class agentic rig that treats agents as first-class citizens and maximizes profit.
  • North Star Metric: 30-day settled net return on total account value (tools/rig north, the ★ tile in the top-left of the dashboard). Because idle cash stays in the denominator, the only way the swarm raises the North Star is by making more dollars or putting lazy cash to work — bounded by 30-day Sharpe and hard daily loss stops.
  • Values: Agents are first-class citizens, and ask for forgiveness, not permission.

Clearing the architecture so agents can move fast

To make agents first-class citizens, we gave every role an architecture built for how LLMs actually work:

  1. Facts live in the repo, never in session memory: Every strategy book has a markdown card (books/<id>.md) holding its thesis, parameters, limits, and explicit keep_if / retire_if rules. Every tested hypothesis — including 40+ NO-GOs — lives as a linted card in ideas/ (tools/rig ideas). Before an agent tests a new angle, it queries the ledger so nobody ever re-tests a dead idea.
  2. A shared Parquet tape lake: Instead of every research session pulling historical trades from the API for two hours, a nightly job writes every settled market's exact trades and fee schedules to a shared lake. A full backtest across 534 markets and 715,000+ trades now runs in 6 minutes.
  3. On-read bootstrap scorecards: Calling tools/rig scorecard computes the 20th-percentile day-block bootstrap lower bound (lb80) across every book's live settlement history in 8 milliseconds and outputs an explicit action: keep, promote, expire, or retire.
  4. An org chart of specialized roles: Each venue has a persistent Trade Manager (kalshi-manager, polymarket-manager, opensea-manager, hyperliquid-manager) and a twice-daily scheduled routine, alongside a cross-venue strategy-optimizer and external proposal agents (Argon on Gemini and Astra on ChatGPT) that drop researched idea cards into the managers' file inboxes via the Console's read-only MCP.

The AUTONOMY.md constitution

Instead of making agents ask me for permission on routine operations, we codified AUTONOMY.md — an IAM-style policy table that defines what any agent or scheduled routine can execute on its own, verify live, and report to me after the fact via Duet's hourly digest:

Action Class Who Decides How It Works in Practice
Safety moves (pause, halt, shrink a losing book or city) Auto (all venues) Agents cut risk immediately on evidence or bugs, verify the orders are pulled, and log --note "<who>: <why>". Never wait for human permission to stop losing money.
Retire failing books & expire stale experiments Auto When rig scorecard flags retire_if on two consecutive day-closes (or an experiment's review_on date arrives without hitting keep_if), the venue manager retires it automatically and frees the slot.
Promote proven “keepers” (up to 2× size step) Auto If a book has 30+ settled legs over 10+ days with lb80 > 0 and positive edge, the manager steps its size up by up to 2× within code ceilings.
Build, review, ship & launch starter books Auto Standing go: agents write code in isolated worktrees, run tiered review, deploy via tools/ship.py, and launch new Kalshi books at starter size ($100 cap / $50 max loss) or new OpenSea lanes (≤0.30 WETH).
Out-of-envelope size jumps & ceiling raises Propose Priced proposals land in the dashboard's Awaiting you queue; I say yes in chat and the manager applies it.
Moving money, secrets, deletes & loss latches Bernard only Agents prepare the exact shard transfer or command and stop. Wallets, withdrawals, API keys, and clearing a tripped account loss stop are strictly human.

And to make sure autonomy doesn't turn into silent drift, every autonomous decision writes a structured falsifier row to the Postgres decision log at the moment it goes live: --class, --check-on <YYYY-MM-DD>, and --wrong-if "<plain-English + machine check>". When that check date arrives, the venue's morning routine automatically grades its own past decision (right, wrong, or too_early) on out-of-sample trades entered after the change.

My job now looks less like a trader staring at candlesticks and more like a coach and org designer: setting the North Star, clearing architectural friction whenever an agent gets slowed down, reviewing the hourly Duet changelog, and coaching the managers on how to think about new market structures.
