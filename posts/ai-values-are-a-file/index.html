I Read the Files That Taught AI to Recommend — and Learned It Mostly Didn't

Bernard Huang
July 26, 2026 · 9 min read

Last week I trained a language model on my own Google history to see how beliefs get into a model. That covered pretraining. But pretraining doesn't explain why a chatbot refuses your question, apologizes too much, or decides whether to name a product when you ask for a recommendation. That comes from a later stage — preference training, or RLHF — and it always felt like the black-box part of the pipeline.

Then I learned something almost nobody outside AI labs seems to know: the actual files are public. Anthropic's original preference dataset is on Hugging Face. So is Nvidia's. You can open them like spreadsheets and read, row by row, the human judgments that became a chatbot's values. I went in with one question — how does an AI learn to recommend products? — and came out with three answers I didn't expect.

TL;DR

I read the two most important public preference datasets — Anthropic's hh-rlhf (2022) and Nvidia's HelpSteer2 (2024) — end to end. The definition of a "good answer" changed between them, and you can watch it happen.

  • A refusal is a file where the competent answer was marked rejected — you can read the exact rows
  • The training rubric itself evolved: 2022 asked "which answer is safer?"; 2024 grades five dimensions of competence
  • Product recommendation flipped sides: hedging won in 2022, naming names wins in 2024
  • The surprise: recommendation queries are ~0.2% of preference data — the behavior every brand obsesses over was barely trained at all

The files are public

Two datasets anchor this story, and both are a click away:

  • Anthropic/hh-rlhf (published 2022–2023) — 169,000 rows of the data behind the original "helpful and harmless" assistant research. Each row: one conversation, two candidate responses, and a human's verdict on which was better.
  • nvidia/HelpSteer2 (2024) — ~21,000 rows built to train reward models. Each row: a prompt, a response, and five graded scores from human annotators.

These aren't documentation about training. They're the training material itself — the same kind of data that, at massive scale, turned raw autocomplete engines into the assistants you use daily. Reading them feels like finding the props department of a magic show.
